What data centers are actually permitted in your county.

There is a great deal of noise about data centers right now and very little that anyone can check. Groundwork reads the permits. For every county it tracks, it publishes what operators have actually filed with the regulator — how many sites, how many backup generators, how much they are permitted to emit — each figure sourced to the filing it came from. No press releases, and no panic.

[01] Your county

Start with where you live.

Read the map as a floor, not a census. Coverage is uneven by design of the source, not by design of ours: air permitting is a state function, so there is no national permit list. The national layer is EPA's registry, where the industry code is self-reported — which is why Texas shows only 61 facilities here despite being one of the largest data center markets in the country. An empty county means nobody has filed under a code we can see, never that nothing is there.

Of the 396 sites located precisely enough to check, 15 sit inside a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area and 162 draw from basins WRI rates high or extremely high for water stress. Virginia — the densest cluster on the map — has zero in a flood zone; its exposure is water.

    [03] The threshold

    Permits stop just short of the line.

    Virginia air permits tabulate how much nitrogen oxide a facility may emit each year. At 100 tons a year a new source becomes a major source, which brings a stricter review and a public participation process. Of the 155 data center permits where that figure is readable, here is where they land.

    Under 25 48
    25 – 50 13
    50 – 75 13
    75 – 90 9
    90 – 99.99 70
    100 and over 2

    Tons of NOx per year permitted, per facility. Read from the emissions table of each permit and labelled probable — a figure parsed from a PDF, and a permitted ceiling rather than measured emissions.

    What this is not

    It is not evidence of wrongdoing. Designing a facility to stay under a regulatory threshold is lawful, ordinary engineering, and every industry does it. These are also backup generators — permitted to run during outages and for testing, typically tens of hours a year.

    Groundwork takes no view on any individual application, and does not claim any single permit was sized to avoid scrutiny.

    What it does show

    The distribution is not what engineering need alone would produce. 70 permits land in the last ten tons below the line; 2 cross it. A threshold that was incidental would not bend the curve this hard.

    Whatever else is true, the review process that 100 tons is meant to trigger is being triggered 2 times, in the densest data center market on earth.

    [05] Methodology

    How this is built, and where it is weak.

    Sources

    • VA DEQ data center permits — 201 permits, each PDF read for address, equipment and permitted NOx.
    • EPA ECHO — 237 facilities in other states, by industry code and operator name.
    • FEMA NFHL and WRI Aqueduct 4.0 — flood zone and water stress, by exact location.

    Limits

    • A floor, not a census. Permitting is a state function. Depth varies by what each state publishes — every county page says which.
    • Virginia is deepest by accident of disclosure, not importance. It is the only state with a data-center-specific permit list.
    • Permits are ceilings, not measurements, and approval is not construction.
    • 91 sites have no verified coordinate and are off the map by choice. A county centroid would imply precision the filing does not support.
    • 457 facilities were excluded — they carry the data-processing industry code but nothing identifies them as data centers.

    Spotted an error?

    Fuzzy-matched fields are labelled as such and some will be wrong. Open an issue — corrections are tracked in public and noted on the page.
