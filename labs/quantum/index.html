[01] Four businesses

"Quantum" is not one market. It is four, on four different clocks.

They share the physics and almost nothing else: different buyers, different capital needs, different timelines to revenue. Two of them sell products today.

Computing

Canonical POV

Computing gets the headlines and most of the capital. Security and sensing get paying customers first. In 2025 the industry raised more than twice what it sold, so the question for any quantum company is which buyer has a budget today.

[02] The physics, by hand

One idea explains the opportunity and the difficulty: interference.

Not "a qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time." Four experiments, about two minutes, and you will understand what founders mean by coherence, fidelity and error correction.

2.1

Fire electrons at two slits

Each electron lands as a single dot. Fire enough and stripes appear, with bands where almost nothing lands. That only happens with waves. Now switch on the detector, which records which slit each electron used. The stripes vanish. Measuring the path destroys the interference, and anything that bumps a qubit, such as heat or vibration, does the same thing by accident.

0 electrons

Simulated with a simplified two-slit model.

2.2

Add the arrows, then square

Two routes lead to the same spot, and each alone gives a 25% chance. Ordinary odds would add to 50%. Quantum mechanics adds amplitudes, arrows with a direction called phase, then squares the result. Turn the phase and the same two routes give anything from 100% to 0%. A quantum algorithm is a way of setting the phases so wrong answers cancel.

Chance an electron lands here
100%
Ordinary odds would always say 50%
2.3

Add a qubit, double the possibilities

A machine with n qubits works with 2n amplitudes at once. That is where the potential power comes from, and why ordinary computers cannot simulate one past a few dozen qubits.

1.1 × 109
possibilities in play
2.4

Why 99.9% isn't good enough

Errors compound. Pick how accurate each operation is and how many the calculation needs. Useful algorithms need millions to billions, which is why error correction, combining many physical qubits into one reliable logical qubit, matters more than qubit count.

Chance the whole calculation comes out clean
37%

Simplified: assumes independent errors and no correction.

Canonical POV

Interference is the product, and the environment is constantly trying to destroy it. When a founder quotes a qubit count, ask for fidelity, coherence time and logical error rate. Qubit count alone tells you very little.

[03] The gap is closing

The machines are getting bigger. The requirement is falling faster.

In 2012 the best estimate for breaking RSA-2048, the encryption behind much of the internet, was about a billion physical qubits. By 2025 it was under a million. The largest machines grew from a handful of qubits to about a thousand over the same period.

3.1

Estimated qubits to break RSA-2048 vs largest machines built

Needed: published estimates for RSA-2048 Built: largest gate-based processor Hollow: unreviewed or not yet a computer

Needed: Fowler et al. (2012), Gidney & Ekerå (2019), Gidney (2025), a 2026 preprint claiming under 100,000. Built: IBM (2016, 2021, 2022, 2023), Google Sycamore (2019), Caltech 6,100-atom array (2025, trapped atoms, not yet a computer). Counts ignore quality, which matters as much as size.

3.2

Built today vs what useful jobs need

Built: Quantinuum Helios, Google Willow, IBM Nighthawk, IBM Condor, Caltech atom array. Needed: Google Quantum AI (Mar 2026) for 256-bit elliptic-curve keys, Gidney (May 2025) for RSA-2048, Google / PRX Quantum (2021) for the FeMoco catalyst. Estimates assume about 0.1% physical error rates.

Canonical POV

The estimate has dropped by roughly an order of magnitude every few years, from better algorithms and error-correcting codes, not just bigger chips. Plan for the curve, not today's number. That is the urgency behind everything in the next section.

[04] Security

The threat doesn't need a quantum computer to exist yet.

An attacker can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it later. Michele Mosca's rule: if the years your data must stay secret plus the years it takes to migrate exceed the years until a code-breaking machine arrives, you are already late. NIST finalized the replacement standards in 2024.

4.2

You can't replace cryptography you can't find

The algorithms are free and standardized. The hard part is that most organizations have no inventory of where cryptography lives.

What teams think about
  • Websites (HTTPS)
  • VPNs
What migration actually has to find
  • Certificates and keys
  • Internal services and APIs
  • Embedded and IoT devices
  • Mobile apps
  • Software libraries
  • Backups and archives
  • Vendor systems
  • Hardware security modules
  • Crypto wallets and signatures

Crypto is a special case. Google's March 2026 estimate covers the signatures that secure Bitcoin and Ethereum. Bitcoin merged BIP-360, its first quantum-resistant address type, this year. The harder problem is governance: who decides what happens to coins whose keys are already exposed.

[05] Sensing

The bug is the feature.

The sensitivity that ruins quantum computers is exactly what makes quantum sensors precise. A computer tries to block out the world. A sensor tries to feel it.

In a quantum computer

Heat, vibration and stray fields are noise to be shielded against. Goal: block the outside world.

In a quantum sensor

A tiny magnetic field or a change in gravity is the signal to be measured. Goal: feel the outside world.

Atomic clocks have been products for decades. The new wave is navigation that works when GPS is jammed, plus magnetometers and gravity sensors. Defense and aerospace account for an estimated 60–70% of quantum sensor revenue today.

The first quantum products with real buyers are instruments. The test is field data at the right size, weight, power and cost, against the best classical sensor rather than an old one. Lab precision that disappears in a moving vehicle is not a product.

[06] The enabling stack

Nobody knows which qubit wins. Sell to all of them.

There are five serious ways to build a qubit, and none has won. Every one of them needs control electronics, cryogenics or lasers, photonics, calibration software and fast classical processors to decode errors.

[07] What we're watching

What would change our view.

  1. Logical qubits doing real work. 100+ error-corrected qubits running a useful algorithm, independently verified, would pull computing forward.
  2. A peer-reviewed RSA estimate under 100,000 qubits. That would compress every post-quantum migration deadline.
  3. A post-quantum vendor with recurring revenue independent of the big platforms. The test of whether migration is a company or a feature.
  4. Quantum navigation fielded on a drone. At drone-sized weight, power and cost, against the best classical inertial sensor.
  5. The National Quantum Initiative reauthorization signed into law. Both versions passed committee in 2026; the Senate bill directs a federal post-quantum migration strategy.
Methodology, caveats and sources

Simulations

The two-slit simulator samples from a simplified model: two overlapping single-slit patterns with interference when unwatched, and two separate piles when watched. The error calculator uses success ≈ (1 − error rate)operations, assuming independent errors and no correction. The Mosca calculator uses example inputs; nobody knows when a code-breaking machine arrives.

Estimates

Qubit requirements depend on assumed physical error rates (about 0.1% here), code choice and runtime. Estimates keep falling as algorithms and codes improve. Counts of machines built ignore quality.
